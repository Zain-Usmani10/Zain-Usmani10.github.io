01 / Flight hardware

Interstellar: FPGA flight computer

Interstellar · FPGA flight computer · Hack the Nest 2026 · built with Abderrahmene, Tanzim and Affan, fellow ECE students · November 2026

A laptop plans the mission from NASA/JPL Horizons data. An FPGA flies it from a 28-byte plan, including through a deliberate link blackout. I built the flight computer in Verilog, the serial protocol and ESP32S3 bridge, and the Python backend and orbit maths.

On the board · Verilog, DE10-Lite

  • Mission sequencer: a 7-state machine (IDLE, LOADED, ARMED, RUNNING, SAFE, COMPLETE, ABORTED) on an Intel MAX 10, booting from its own flash. Each burn fires at most once, at or after its scheduled tick, and deducts from an integer fuel ledger in grams.
  • Triple modular redundancy: state, burn record, fuel and burn ticks stored three times and read through a majority voter. Proven on the real board: SW0 corrupted one copy, it was outvoted and the mission completed; SW1 made all three disagree and the board latched SAFE with no capture burn.
  • Autonomy: the board never waits on the laptop to keep flying. A link watchdog calls the link lost after 2 s without the twice-a-second keep-alive, and only drives the displays.
  • Fixed mission clock: 100 ticks per second for every plan, 5 s countdown, 30 s of flight, 3 s tail. Days to arrival in integer-only arithmetic, so board and laptop agree to the day with no floating point on the FPGA.
  • Cockpit: six seven-segment digits plus the 640×480 VGA cockpit emulated above.

On the wire and the ground · protocol, Python

  • Binary protocol at 115200 baud: A5 5A sync, version, type, 16-bit sequence, 32-bit session, length, payload and CRC-16/CCITT-FALSE. A 28-byte plan in, a 40-byte STATUS out ten times a second.
  • Stop-and-wait, idempotent commands: one in flight, each answered by a 16-byte ACK; a repeat with the same sequence is acknowledged but never applied twice, so a retry can't fire a burn twice.
  • Golden frames shared byte for byte between the Python tests and the Verilog testbenches. The XIAO ESP32S3 bridge only passes bytes and is checked by a link probe before every hardware run.
  • Orbit maths: Lambert transfers on Horizons state vectors, burns from patched conics, fuel from the rocket equation; 13 routes and 38 flight options, each re-flown with SciPy DOP853 and Radau to arrive within 10 km.
  • Backend: FastAPI and WebSockets with one thread owning the serial port, a board simulator, the 25–85 % blackout with labelled estimates, and over 400 tests.

O-Hive: depth network to FPGA

FPGA and Hardware Engineering Co-op · O-Hive, Windsor, ON · April – August 2026

O-Hive builds humanoid cobot arms. I moved their depth network from a cloud GPU toward on-board hardware: quantized it, generated RTL, fixed synthesis so buffers map to BRAM, brought up the Kria KV260 and camera, and designed the arm power distribution. I also built the vision pipeline. Team of three.

Bringing up the Kria KV260

13 MP camera into one buffer, shared by the FPGA depth accelerator and a CPU tracker.

Shared NV12 buffer

Set up an Ubuntu 22.04 Docker workspace for the cross-compilation toolchain, then brought up the KV260 (quad-core Cortex-A53, 4 GB) and ran the cross-compiled binaries in its embedded Linux.

Raw 4208×3120 NV12 frames from the AR1335 failed to capture at first; dmesg and GStreamer debug logs traced it to a mismatch between the format the pipeline requested and what the sensor driver output.

NV12 already stores luminance as its own plane, two thirds of every frame. My C++ fork sends that plane straight to the CPU for SVO, and only the FPGA's copy gets converted to RGB.

Arm power distribution

Distribution and regen-clamp schematics for the cobot arms, reviewed over three rounds.

Load sizing and protection

Each arm runs four actuators (three Robstride 04, one Robstride 03) on a 48 V bus. A load table of every actuator's peak current summed to 168 A per arm; I rated the distribution for 240 A, picked the main breaker, and checked the ampacity of every conductor segment.

When an arm decelerates or is back-driven by a person, the motors become generators and push energy onto the bus, which a DC supply can't sink. ODrive regen clamps switch in a brake resistor once the bus rises past threshold.

One review revision added a breaker per actuator branch, so a single shorted actuator trips only its own branch, not the whole arm.

03 / Motorsport

Formula Electric SAE

Two roles · Lancer Motorsport, Windsor, ON · December 2024 – present

Fully electric race car, 120 km/h. I own the sensor drivers and telemetry, and lead the autonomy team of five engineering students.

Drivers, telemetry, signal integrity
Embedded C · SPI · I2C · Qt · SQLite · CI
  • Real-time telemetry sampling 11 vehicle-dynamics channels at 50 Hz (550 samples per second on a 20 ms period), logged to onboard storage and shown on a Qt dashboard on RPi OS.
  • Low-level C drivers: SPI at 1 MHz for the 6-axis IMU and the 12-bit ADC on the suspension potentiometers, I2C in 400 kHz fast mode for temperature and pressure.
  • I2C reads failed on the full vehicle harness. On the scope, SDA and SCL rose in about 450 ns, past the 300 ns fast-mode limit: harness capacitance against 10 kΩ pull-ups. Swapped to 2.2 kΩ, measured rise times back under 300 ns, and the errors stopped.
  • Dashboard memory climbed over long sessions: the driver allocated a read buffer per sample and never freed it on the error path. Gave every buffer a single owner, freed on every return path, and memory stayed flat.
  • CI gate: every merge to main must pass 14 hardware unit tests (one per channel, SPI init, I2C init, logging integrity). It caught an SPI configuration change that would have broken IMU reads.

Cone detection to steering control
Computer vision · C++ control · Linux services
  • Leading a team of 5 engineering students in research and development: sourcing and labelling cone datasets, training detection models, benchmarking navigation algorithms before committing to the car.
  • Python vision that finds the blue and yellow cones marking the track, plus obstacles, from a 30 fps camera: a 33 ms budget per frame.

04 / Research

Gait classification from a wearable IMU

Embedded Systems Research Intern · Micro-Nano Mechatronics Lab, Windsor · May – December 2025

IMU firmware, data pipeline and classifier. Co-authoring the biomechanics paper.

  • ESP32 Bluetooth Low Energy firmware in C++ streaming 6-axis IMU data at 50 Hz, one sample every 20 ms.
  • Early recordings had gaps and bursts: when the radio was busy, notifications queued faster than connection events could send them. Added a firmware ring buffer to ride out latency spikes and a sequence number on every packet, so lost samples get flagged instead of quietly stitched over.
  • Recorded 8 participants; Python sanitization used the sequence numbers to flag gaps and dropped readings saturated at full scale (about 5 %).
  • Features from 16,000 samples with pandas and NumPy; XGBoost and KNN reached 78.9 F1 under 5-fold cross-validation, against 61 for a threshold baseline.
